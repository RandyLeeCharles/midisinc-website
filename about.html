About the MIDI SINC Project

Combining Traditional Organ Building with Modern Technology

Hello, my name is Randy Charles, and I am the creator of the MIDI SINC Project.

MIDI SINC was founded on a simple idea: the knowledge, designs, electronics, and technology needed to build and restore mechanical musical instruments should be available to everyone.

This project combines traditional organ-building principles with modern technologies including 3D printing, CAD design, custom electronics, MIDI control systems, printed circuit board development, and open-source software.

The goal is not simply to build a single organ. The goal is to create and freely share designs, techniques, electronics, software, and documentation that can help hobbyists, makers, experimenters, educators, woodworkers, restorers, and future organ builders.

What Is MIDI SINC?

While the project is often described as a 3D-printed pipe organ project, MIDI SINC is actually the development of an entire open-source organ system.

Major development areas include:

  • 3D-printed organ pipe development
  • Traditional organ pipe design
  • Custom MIDI player systems
  • MIDI Solenoid Drivers (MSDs)
  • Controller electronics
  • PCB design and testing
  • Firmware development
  • Musical instrument automation
  • Glockenspiel control systems
  • Mechanical actuator systems
  • Open-source documentation

The objective is to create a complete system that can be used to operate pipe organs, glockenspiels, and other mechanical musical instruments while making the designs available to the community.

Traditional Craft Meets Modern Manufacturing

An important goal of the project is combining proven organ-building methods with modern manufacturing technology.

Although MIDI SINC includes extensive use of 3D printing, the project is not intended to replace traditional woodworking techniques.

Many of the pipe designs developed through this project can be constructed either as 3D-printed pipes or as traditional wooden pipes using the same dimensions and design principles.

This allows builders to choose the construction method that best fits their tools, skills, and interests.

What Parts Are 3D Printed?

One of the long-term goals of MIDI SINC is the development of what may become one of the world's first open-source pipe organs making extensive use of 3D-printed components.

Currently, the primary 3D-printed components include:

  • Organ pipes
  • Pipe prototypes and experimental designs
  • Glockenspiel actuator assemblies
  • Custom brackets and fixtures
  • Mechanical prototype components

Many of the larger structural components continue to be constructed using traditional woodworking methods.

These include:

  • Organ frame
  • Windchests
  • Glockenspiel frame
  • Structural assemblies
  • Various support components

This hybrid approach allows each component to be produced using the materials and construction methods best suited to its purpose.

Current Development Limitations

Most of the project's development work is currently performed using consumer-grade 3D printers with build volumes of approximately 11 inches x 11 inches x 11 inches.

These machines have proven extremely useful for developing smaller pipes and components. However, larger organ pipes often must be produced in multiple sections and assembled after printing.

While functional, sectional construction can introduce challenges involving alignment, airflow consistency, resonance, voicing, and structural integrity.

Future development will focus on expanding large-format printing capabilities to allow longer pipes to be manufactured in single continuous sections.

Open Source Commitment

The foundation of MIDI SINC is the belief that knowledge should be shared.

As designs are validated and documented, resources will be released whenever possible, including:

  • Pipe drawings
  • Dimensional plans
  • STL files
  • CAD models
  • Electronics schematics
  • PCB layouts
  • Firmware
  • Software
  • Construction notes
  • Testing results
  • Lessons learned

The objective is to build a growing library of resources that others can use, modify, improve, and expand.

Why I Continue

Over the years I have personally invested thousands of dollars and countless hours into researching, designing, testing, building, and refining these systems.

As a retiree, progress is sometimes limited by both time and budget, but the project continues moving forward because I believe these resources can help make organ building, experimentation, and restoration more accessible to future generations.

Every successful prototype, every electronics revision, every software update, and every tested pipe becomes another piece of information that can be shared with the community.

Looking Ahead

The future of MIDI SINC is not just one instrument.

The vision is a growing collection of freely available designs, electronics, software, construction methods, and educational resources that can benefit hobbyists, makers, experimenters, educators, woodworkers, and organ builders around the world.

Thank you for visiting and following the project.

I hope the information shared here inspires others to build, experiment, learn, and help preserve the fascinating world of mechanical music.

Randy Charles
